# MOWES Competitor Analysis
### Where a free, private, beginner-friendly workout app can win
*Researched July 2026. Sources listed at the bottom. Kept private — this is a working document, not part of the app.*

---

## Bottom line
The fitness-app market is crowded but built around the **opposite** of what MOWES stands for: almost every app wants an account, sells or shares your data, and hides the useful parts behind a monthly fee. The people MOWES cares about most — **true beginners, seniors, folks doing gentle rehab, and budget home-trainers** — are the ones the big apps ignore, talk down to, or lock out behind insurance plans. That's the open lane: a genuinely free, no-account, private, offline, plain-and-simple weekly planner is rare enough that it can own this niche.

---

## The landscape

**All-in-one "coach in your pocket" apps** (guided videos, full programs) — bloated, pay-monthly:
- Fitbod (~$16/mo), Freeletics (~$12–15/mo; "free" but paywalls the real content), Peloton App ($13–29/mo), Apple Fitness+ ($10/mo), Centr (~$20/mo), Sweat (~$20/mo). All require an account. **Nike Training Club** is the exception — genuinely free with a full feature set, though it still pushes account creation and shares data.

**Gym lift-loggers** (record sets/reps/weight) — simpler, cheap-or-free:
- Strong (free tier, Pro $5/mo), Hevy (genuinely free, ~14M users, adds a social feed), Jefit (free but ad-supported), **FitNotes** (Android, fully free, no ads, no forced account, stores data on your phone). FitNotes is the closest thing to MOWES's spirit already out there.

**Running/cycling trackers** — free logging, pay for coaching; all need accounts:
- Strava (free tier, ~$12/mo premium), Nike Run Club (free), Garmin Connect (free for Garmin owners).

**Home/beginner (no-equipment) apps** — freemium:
- FitOn (lots free, premium for weekly plans), Home Workout by Leap Fitness (freemium), Nike Training Club.

**Yoga/mobility** — subscription-driven, accounts required:
- Down Dog (~$10/mo), Alo Moves (~$13/mo), Glo (~$30/mo).

**Senior & rehab apps** — mostly locked behind insurance, Medicare, employers, or a clinician:
- SilverSneakers GO (free only if your Medicare plan includes it), Bold and Nymbl (Medicare-Advantage funded), Grow Young Fitness (direct subscription ~$15/mo), Hinge Health ($0 but only through your employer/health plan), Kaia Health (~$15/mo), Physitrack/PhysiApp (free app but needs a clinician to prescribe it). **If you're uninsured or without a plan, most of these are simply unavailable at any price.**

---

## The gaps MOWES can own
- **Privacy is a real hole.** ~75–80% of popular fitness apps share your data with third parties; a Duke study found 79% share data while only 28% of users realize it. Apps that need no account and keep everything on your phone are genuinely rare — almost all are small open-source projects (FitNotes, Feeel, wger). MOWES fits exactly this gap, but as a **friendly, welcoming** app rather than a techie one.
- **"Free" almost never means free.** Only ~1.5% of top health/fitness apps are paid up front — but nearly all the rest are *freemium*: free to download, pay to actually use. Basics get moved behind paywalls (MyFitnessPal famously paywalled its barcode scanner). A truly no-paywall, no-ads app is uncommon.
- **Beginners are actively failed.** An analysis of 50 apps found **73% fail beginners**: 74% use unexplained jargon ("RPE 8," "AMRAP"), 58% of "beginner" tiers aren't actually beginner-level, and most just hand you an exercise library and expect willpower — "which is why most beginners quit within three weeks."
- **Seniors are underserved by design.** Peer-reviewed research and Nielsen Norman Group (18 years of study) confirm fitness apps use fonts too small, tap targets too tiny, and layouts too cluttered for older users, who say the apps feel "made for someone very different than me." The senior apps that do exist are gated behind insurance.
- **People are tired of bloat and forced sign-ups.** Users reject unnecessarily complex apps, praise minimal ones like FitNotes for being fast and private, and reject social feeds as clutter. 89% say they'd switch away from an app whose setup feels overwhelming (one app makes you tap through ~45–50 screens before you can start).
- **Offline matters.** Gym dead zones, outdoor workouts, and bad WiFi make apps freeze mid-session — a common trigger for quitting. Offline-first doubles as a privacy win.

---

## Ways to stand out
1. **Lead with "no sign-up, no account, no email — ever."** Say it plainly on the first screen. It's a trust magnet in a market where ~79% of apps quietly share data.
2. **Zero-friction start.** Plan and log a first workout in seconds — no quiz, no survey, no permissions wall. Beats the "45–50 screen" onboarding that makes 89% of users bail.
3. **Own "the app that doesn't assume you're already fit."** Plain words (no "RPE," no "AMRAP"), short how-tos for each move, gentle beginner suggestions. Targets the 73% of apps that fail beginners.
4. **Design visibly for seniors and shaky eyes.** Big text, big tap targets, high contrast, roomy layout, adjustable font size. Almost no mainstream app does this well.
5. **Be genuinely free — no paywall, no ads — and say so.** Not "free trial," not "freemium." Position against the "paywalled to death" backlash.
6. **Serve the uninsured senior and the home rehab self-trainer** — the people Medicare/employer-gated apps structurally leave out. A gentle, private, free planner for light dumbbells / treadmill / exercise bike fills a real, growing gap (the 55+ active-aging segment is ~$14B and growing fast).
7. **Make offline a headline, not a footnote.** "Works with no internet, anywhere."
8. **Deliberately stay small and calm.** No social feed, no follower counts, no fatigue scores. Market the "no clutter" as the point.
